THE SEPTEMBER FOMC: WHO IS REALLY CONTROLLING THE FED?

Oil is back above $100, the bond market is pressing against a 5% U.S. 10-year yield, the White House wants easier policy, and a new Fed chair is approaching his most politically sensitive decision yet.

THE CENTRAL QUESTIONDoes the Fed control the bond market, or is the bond market now controlling the Fed?

The September meeting is not simply a vote between a hike and a pause. It is a test of whether monetary policy still controls the market's inflation narrative, or whether oil, fiscal policy and the global bond market have begun controlling monetary policy.

The September 16 meeting includes a new Summary of Economic Projections, which turns one rate decision into a combined policy, forecasting and credibility event. August payrolls rose by 162,000, core inflation remained firm, and interest-rate futures moved sharply toward another hike. Yet the tape has not behaved like a simple tightening shock.

The decision is best understood as a four-way constraint problem. Oil-driven inflation, Fed credibility, White House pressure and global bond stress are pulling the reaction function in different directions.

01

ENERGY / INFLATION

OIL HAS RETURNED AS THE INFLATION WILDCARD

De-escalation once looked plausible. Instead, military and shipping risks intensified and crude moved back above $100. That matters because the Fed's inflation problem is no longer purely domestic. Higher energy costs can move through transport, production and inflation expectations even when the initial shock begins overseas.

The first major oil shock of 2026 coincided with an S&P 500 drawdown of more than 5% from its January high. This time the index remained close to its record after the September 11 session, even with crude above $100 and the 10-year yield near 5%. That divergence is information. Earnings, AI capital expenditure, positioning and expectations of an eventual policy response may still be strong enough to absorb the shock.

A rate hike cannot reopen a shipping route. It can only try to stop the oil shock from becoming embedded in broader inflation expectations.

THESIS 01

IS THE OIL NARRATIVE BEING MANAGED?

Claim
War, negotiation and shipping headlines may be used, amplified or timed in ways that influence the inflation narrative through crude.
Mechanism
Headline → crude → inflation expectations → Fed pricing → yields and equities.
What would confirm it
A repeated relationship between political headline timing, crude, breakevens and rapid Fed repricing.
What would falsify it
Oil and inflation expectations move independently of the political and diplomatic headline cycle.
Conventional explanation
Crude is reacting normally to genuine supply and shipping risk in a thin, headline-sensitive market.
02

REACTION FUNCTION

WARSH AND THE PROBLEM OF CREDIBILITY

Kevin Warsh entered the chairmanship trying to define a reaction function that is not mechanically hostage to every decimal point in a single release. The market is now testing that stance. If inflation is firm, payroll growth is strong and futures assign a high probability to a hike, does the Fed have to validate that pricing?

This is where the bond-vigilante framing becomes useful as a mechanism. Large holders, primary dealers and duration sellers do not need to coordinate. Rising yields, weak auction demand and more expensive refinancing can constrain policymakers mechanically.

If the Fed follows futures every time the market aggressively reprices the next meeting, the relationship becomes circular. Are markets predicting the Fed, or pressuring the Fed into validating the prediction?

THESIS 02

WHOSE CREDIBILITY MATTERS?

Claim
The loudest demands for Fed credibility may reflect the incentives of dealers, duration holders, banks and institutions exposed to inflation and term-premium risk.
Mechanism
Bond losses and auction stress → pressure for tighter policy → Fed validates pricing → duration stabilizes.
What would confirm it
The long end calms after Fed validation and punishes a decision that resists market pricing.
What would falsify it
The Fed rejects market pricing and long Treasuries rally anyway.
Conventional explanation
Credibility is simply the public's belief that the Fed will return inflation to target over time.
03

FISCAL / MONETARY COLLISION

THE WHITE HOUSE WANTS THE OPPOSITE

The Trump administration has repeatedly argued for lower interest rates. At the same time, Trump has promoted a proposed $5,000 citizen dividend tied politically to Republican control of Congress. The proposal would require congressional approval and could carry a fiscal cost above $1 trillion.

That creates a policy contradiction. The administration wants easier monetary conditions while floating a fiscal transfer that could increase demand, deficits and the burden on monetary policy. This does not prove the proposal was designed to pressure the Fed. The cleaner mechanism is that fiscal expansion and monetary easing can work against one another when inflation is already above target.

THESIS 03

ARE ELECTION INCENTIVES COLLIDING WITH POLICY?

Claim
Rate-cut pressure and the proposed dividend may reflect increasingly aggressive efforts to improve financial conditions and voter sentiment before the midterms.
Mechanism
Fiscal promise plus easier-rate pressure → demand and asset-price support → inflation and bond-market counterpressure.
What would confirm it
Policy messaging becomes more directly linked to markets, household payments and election outcomes.
What would falsify it
The proposal advances through a conventional funded process and rate rhetoric moderates as the election approaches.
Conventional explanation
The proposal is ordinary campaign politics, while rate preferences reflect a conventional growth argument.
04

POLITICAL SIGNAL

THE MIDTERMS RAISE THE COST OF EVERY DECISION

The Fed's legal mandate does not change because an election is approaching. The political cost of every choice does. A hike can be framed as hurting mortgages and households. A pause can be framed by bond investors as a failure to defend price stability. A cut would immediately raise questions about political influence.

September is therefore a choice among competing credibility risks, not an easy choice between tight and loose policy.

Which credibility risk is the Fed most willing to accept before the midterms?
05

THE LONG END

THE BOND MARKET MAY BE THE REAL CONSTRAINT

Sovereign yields across major markets are at or near multi-decade highs. Japan's benchmark 10-year yield reached 3% for the first time since 1996, while the U.S. 10-year approached 5%.

The Fed controls the overnight policy rate. It does not directly control the 10-year yield. If deficits, Treasury supply, term premium and inflation risk keep pushing long yields higher, a 25-basis-point hike does not guarantee that long rates will fall.

THE OBSERVABLE THRESHOLDCan the U.S. 10-year sustainably break and hold above 5%?
06

OFFICIAL SUPPORT

WHAT THE TREASURY BUYBACK ACTUALLY TOLD US

Treasury announced in August that the maximum size of long-end liquidity-support buybacks would rise from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation. Bessent then said the program could grow beyond $4 billion. Two senior Treasury officials also indicated that the department could draw on its near $1 trillion General Account to help fund the expanded purchases.

That combination changed the market's perceived range. The official floor was $4 billion, but the General Account signaled far greater capacity and encouraged expectations to stretch toward $7 billion to $8 billion, with some estimates reaching $10 billion. Treasury ultimately announced a $6 billion cap.

The sequence was not an official $4 billion to $8 billion to $6 billion plan. It was an expectation path: a $4 billion floor, the signal of deeper Treasury firepower, an $8 billion market extrapolation, and a $6 billion announced operation. That path gave Treasury a way to observe how much support the long end expected before committing to the final size.

The decisive evidence is what happens after the operation. If yields refuse to fall despite larger buyback rhetoric, the long end is saying the support is insufficient.

THESIS 04

WAS THE BUYBACK A TRIAL BALLOON?

Claim
The path from a $4 billion floor to an $8 billion market expectation, followed by a $6 billion cap, may have tested how much official support the long end required.
Mechanism
TGA firepower and larger-buyback signals → dealer positioning and duration demand → yield response.
What would confirm it
Buybacks rise when yields approach politically sensitive thresholds and rhetoric escalates with liquidity stress.
What would falsify it
Sizes and timing follow a stable pre-announced framework with no relationship to yield stress.
Conventional explanation
Treasury is improving liquidity in off-the-run securities through its stated debt-management program.

Danger

Constructive

Danger

03

MARKET AS LIE DETECTOR

THE REAL FOMC TEST

Do not judge the meeting only by the policy-rate headline. Watch how the cross-asset system responds.

HIKE + STABLE LONG END

Warsh wins the first round if the dollar firms, oil cools and equities digest the move.

HIKE + 10Y ABOVE 5%

The policy rate is not the dominant problem. Fiscal risk, oil and term premium are winning.

HOLD + BOND RALLY

The claim that markets forced the Fed to validate pricing materially weakens.

HOLD + YIELD SURGE

The pressure mechanism becomes self-reinforcing without requiring coordination.

THE BOTTOM LINE

THE FED IS CHOOSING WHICH CONSTRAINT TO RESPECT.

The September meeting is not simply a vote between a hike and a pause. It is a test of whether the Fed still sets the terms of the inflation debate, or whether oil, fiscal policy, election politics and the global bond market have begun setting those terms for the Fed.

The headline gives the decision. The cross-asset reaction gives the verdict.
